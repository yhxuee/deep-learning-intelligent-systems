Sections 6.2–6.4 answered: *what does one run cost?* This section asks a different question: *if we can afford several possible runs, which one should we choose?* The bridge is a measured relationship between resources and held-out loss.

Keep the logical order visible:

$$
\text{measure runs}\longrightarrow\text{fit a curve}\longrightarrow
\text{predict an unseen run}\longrightarrow\text{optimize the fitted curve}.
$$

Only the final optimization is pure mathematics. Whether the fitted curve describes the next run is an empirical question.

## 6.5.1 Loss is a power law in scale

In several controlled model families and training regimes, held-out language-model loss has been well approximated by power laws over substantial measured ranges **[E]**. [Kaplan et al. (2020)](/references/#kaplan-2020) varied one resource while attempting not to let the others become the bottleneck and fitted

$$
L(N)\approx\Big(\frac{N_c}{N}\Big)^{\alpha_N},\quad L(D)\approx\Big(\frac{D_c}{D}\Big)^{\alpha_D},\quad L(C_{\min})\approx\Big(\frac{C_c}{C_{\min}}\Big)^{\alpha_C},
$$

with $\alpha_N\approx0.076$, $\alpha_D\approx0.095$ and $\alpha_C\approx0.050$, where $N$ counts non-embedding parameters. Earlier work had reported power-law-like learning curves in several domains ([Hestness et al. 2017](/references/#hestness-2017)).

Why is a small exponent still meaningful? If $L\propto N^{-\alpha}$, multiplying $N$ by a factor $r$ multiplies loss by $r^{-\alpha}$. With $\alpha=0.076$, doubling gives $2^{-0.076}\approx0.949$: about a 5.1% reduction, not a fixed subtraction. On log--log axes,

$$
\ln L=\text{constant}-\alpha\ln N,
$$

so the exponent is the negative slope. A straight line on this plot is evidence for a power law only over the range actually plotted.

A pure $AN^{-\alpha}$ curve tends to zero, but next-token NLL cannot fall below the conditional entropy of the data-generating distribution when evaluated honestly (Unit 01, Corollary 1.1). [Hoffmann et al. (2022)](/references/#hoffmann-2022) therefore fitted parameters and data jointly with an explicit asymptote:

$$
L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}} .
$$

The intended interpretation is:

- $E$: the asymptotic floor of this fitted loss surface;
- $A/N^\alpha$: excess loss associated with finite model size;
- $B/D^\beta$: excess loss associated with finite training data.

Their published “approach 3” fit is $E=1.69$, $A=406.4$, $B=410.7$, $\alpha=0.34$, $\beta=0.28$, in nats per token on their data. Calling $E$ “irreducible loss” is convenient but stronger than the fit alone establishes. A finite-range regression can absorb architecture limits, optimization error or distribution choices into $E$; it equals the true entropy floor only under additional assumptions and sufficient asymptotic evidence.

The constants—and possibly the functional form—depend on the corpus, tokenizer, model family, optimizer, schedule and measurement range. What transfers safely is not a numerical ratio but a **method**: posit a surface, fit it on controlled runs, validate it on unseen runs, and report where it fails.

:::note[Three floors that are easy to confuse]
1. **Data entropy:** a property of the evaluation distribution.
2. **Best loss of a restricted model family and training process:** may sit above entropy.
3. **Fitted $E$:** an extrapolated parameter estimated from finite observations.

They can coincide in an ideal limit, but a regression output does not prove that they do.
:::

## 6.5.2 Fitting a law and predicting with it

Fitting $L=E+AN^{-\alpha}$ to a handful of measurements is a nonlinear regression because $E$ appears inside the logarithm. For a **fixed candidate** $E<\min_i L_i$,

$$
\ln(L_i-E)=\ln A-\alpha\ln N_i
$$

is linear in $\ln N_i$. A simple algorithm is therefore:

1. choose a grid of candidate $E$ values below the smallest observed loss;
2. for each $E$, fit intercept $\ln A$ and slope $-\alpha$ by ordinary least squares in log space;
3. transform predictions back to loss space;
4. choose the candidate with the smallest declared error, here squared error in $L$.

This is the educational method in `spine/stage06_scaling.py`. The tests verify that it recovers synthetic parameters generated by its own assumed form. That is a software and identifiability check under ideal data, not evidence that real training runs obey the form.

With few points, $E$ and $\alpha$ can trade off: a lower floor plus a steeper exponent may resemble a higher floor plus a shallower exponent across the observed interval. Residual plots, repeated seeds, alternative point subsets chosen in advance and uncertainty intervals are therefore more informative than reporting one best-fit tuple to many decimal places.

A scaling law earns its keep by **out-of-sample prediction**: fit on smaller runs, predict a larger run *before* training it, then compare. The procedure is Unit 12's preregistration in miniature. Save not only the predicted value, but also the training recipe, included points, fitting objective and tolerance. Otherwise a researcher can unknowingly change the question after seeing the answer.

Worked Example 6.6 does this for the spine model. Its miss shows that the registered fit did not transport to width 48 under that protocol. The residual's size and direction can motivate a new hypothesis, but the same width-48 result cannot both create and independently confirm that hypothesis.

:::note[Claim check]
- **Claim.** A power law fitted on small models predicts the held-out loss of a larger one.
- **Assumptions.** The same data distribution and an equally well-tuned recipe at every size, and no change of regime between the fitted range and the target (§6.7.2).
- **Test.** Write the prediction to disk before training the larger model, then compare (Worked Example 6.6; Experiment 6.4).
- **Limitation.** In Worked Example 6.6 the registered prediction missed by 0.12 nats/token; the curvature is consistent with a regime change but does not by itself identify whether capacity, optimization or another factor caused it. The constants also belong to one recipe and dataset (§6.7.5).
:::


## 6.5.3 The compute-optimal allocation

Suppose, for the moment, that the fitted surface is correct and training cost is exactly $C=6ND$. The budget does not allow $N$ and $D$ to vary independently: choosing $N$ fixes

$$
D=\frac{C}{6N}.
$$

A larger model therefore receives fewer training tokens. The optimization balances two penalties: $AN^{-\alpha}$ falls as $N$ grows, while $BD^{-\beta}$ rises because $D$ shrinks.

**Proposition 6.2.** Minimizing $AN^{-\alpha} + BD^{-\beta}$ subject to $6ND = C$ gives
$$
N^\star = G\Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}},\qquad D^\star = \frac{C}{6N^\star},\qquad G = \Big(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}}.
$$

*Proof, one transformation at a time.* Substitute the budget into the loss and drop the constant $E$, which cannot change the minimizer:

$$
f(N)=AN^{-\alpha}
+B\left(\frac{C}{6N}\right)^{-\beta}
=AN^{-\alpha}+B(C/6)^{-\beta}N^\beta.
$$

Differentiate:

$$
f'(N)=-\alpha AN^{-\alpha-1}
+\beta B(C/6)^{-\beta}N^{\beta-1}.
$$

At an interior optimum, the decrease obtained from enlarging the model equals the increase caused by reducing its data. Setting $f'(N)=0$ and multiplying by $N^{\alpha+1}$ gives

$$
N^{\alpha+\beta}
=\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}(C/6)^\beta.
$$

Taking the $(\alpha+\beta)$th root gives the stated $N^\star$; substituting it into $D=C/(6N)$ gives $D^\star$. To verify a minimum, set $u=\ln N$. Then

$$
f(e^u)=Ae^{-\alpha u}+B(C/6)^{-\beta}e^{\beta u},
$$

whose second derivative is positive for all $u$ because $A,B,\alpha,\beta>0$. Thus it is strictly convex in log-size and has one minimum. $\blacksquare$

The stationary condition also gives an interpretable balance:

$$
\alpha\frac{A}{(N^\star)^\alpha}
=\beta\frac{B}{(D^\star)^\beta}.
$$

The two excess-loss terms need not be equal; their exponent-weighted marginal contributions are.

The exponents control how the optimum moves. With $\alpha=0.34$ and $\beta=0.28$,

$$
N^\star\propto C^{0.28/0.62}\approx C^{0.45},\qquad
D^\star\propto C^{0.34/0.62}\approx C^{0.55}.
$$

Thus this particular fit says parameters and tokens should grow at similar—not identical—rates, and tokens per parameter should change slowly with budget. Hoffmann et al.'s other estimation approaches produced exponents nearer 0.5/0.5 and motivated the widely quoted roughly 20-tokens-per-parameter rule at the Chinchilla scale ([Hoffmann et al. 2022](/references/#hoffmann-2022)) **[E]**. Approach 3 itself gives a different ratio in Worked Example 6.7. A rule of thumb without the fit and scale that produced it is incomplete.

Kaplan et al.'s earlier analysis implied $N^\star\propto C^{0.73}$, allocating more of a growing budget to parameters. Hoffmann et al. argued that part of the discrepancy came from how learning-rate schedules were tied to run length and how parameter counts were handled at small scale. The disagreement is a reminder that “compute-optimal” means optimal **under a fitted surface and accounting convention**.

**The fitted optimum is flat in this example.** At $C=5.76\times10^{23}$, the approach-3 surface predicts $N^\star\approx3.2\times10^{10}$, $D^\star\approx3.0\times10^{12}$ and loss 1.931. It predicts 1.937 for the 70-billion-parameter, 1.4-trillion-token allocation—a difference of only 0.006 nats/token despite a 2.2-fold size change (Worked Example 6.7).

Two consequences follow *within this fit*. First, noisy changes in the constants can move the reported argmin a long way while barely changing predicted loss; a reanalysis found the published approach-3 fit inconsistent with the other two approaches and refitted it ([Besiroglu et al. 2024](/references/#besiroglu-2024)) **[E]**. Second, engineering objectives can justify moving along the shallow valley. A smaller model trained on more data may cost nearly the same to train and be cheaper on every later inference request (§6.7.4). “Flat here” is not “flat for every architecture, budget and metric.”

## 6.5.4 ★ Why power laws?

There is no accepted first-principles derivation of language-model scaling laws. The following argument is a **heuristic model**, useful for generating intuition and tests rather than proving the observed exponents **[H]**.

Assume:

1. relevant examples lie near a manifold with intrinsic dimension $d_{\text{int}}$;
2. $N$ effective degrees of freedom divide that manifold into cells of characteristic width $\ell\propto N^{-1/d_{\text{int}}}$;
3. the target is smooth enough that a local piecewise-linear approximation has pointwise error of order $\ell^2$;
4. the loss contribution behaves like squared approximation error, hence order $\ell^4$.

Then

$$
L-E\propto\ell^4\propto N^{-4/d_{\text{int}}}.
$$

A larger intrinsic dimension gives a smaller exponent and slower improvement. [Sharma & Kaplan (2022)](/references/#sharma-2022) develop a related argument and report settings where measured exponents are roughly consistent with separately estimated intrinsic dimensions **[E]**.

Every numbered assumption can fail for real language: the data need not be one smooth manifold, parameter count need not equal effective cells, cross-entropy is not literally squared error, optimization changes with scale, and architecture changes can create regime shifts. The heuristic therefore explains why exponents might differ across domains; it does not supply “the” exponent of intelligence.

## 6.5.5 ★ Parallel training and communication

When one device cannot hold the state or finish quickly enough, work is split across devices **[O]**. The benefit is not free: every split creates values that must be communicated or stages that can wait.

- **Data parallelism.** Each of $p$ devices holds a full model copy and processes a different batch slice; gradients are averaged each step. In an ideal ring all-reduce, each device transfers an amount proportional to $2G(p-1)/p$ for a $G$-byte gradient payload, plus per-message latency. The bandwidth term approaches about twice the gradient size as $p$ grows rather than shrinking to zero.
- **Sharded optimizer and model state.** Partitioning some combination of optimizer moments, gradients and parameters removes redundant copies of the §6.2.4 state, at the price of gathering or reducing values when they are used ([Rajbhandari et al. 2020](/references/#rajbhandari-2020)).
- **Tensor parallelism.** Split individual matrix products. Partial outputs often require collective communication within each layer.
- **Pipeline parallelism.** Put consecutive layer groups on different devices and stream micro-batches through them. Too few micro-batches leave a “bubble” in which stages are idle ([Narayanan et al. 2021](/references/#narayanan-2021)).

Each scheme trades memory per device against communication and idle time. They are commonly combined because they solve different constraints.

**Amdahl's law** gives a first warning. If a fixed fraction $s$ of work is serial and the remaining fraction parallelizes perfectly, speedup on $p$ devices is

$$
S(p)\le\frac{1}{s+(1-s)/p},
$$

which approaches $1/s$. Real distributed training is more complicated: communication can grow with $p$, global batch changes may alter optimization, and some communication overlaps computation. Amdahl's formula is therefore a diagnostic model, not a performance prediction. High MFU requires sufficiently large local products, fast interconnects, balanced stages and careful overlap; overlap is one technique, not an explanation by itself.
