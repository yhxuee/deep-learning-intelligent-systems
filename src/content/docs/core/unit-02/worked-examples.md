Exact arithmetic is recomputed by `labs/test_worked_examples_u01_u02.py`; trained-model results (2.4–2.6) are generated by `spine/stage02_embeddings.py`, with representative results and the complete validation/test comparison checked in `spine/test_spine.py`. ## Worked Example 2.1 — XOR, impossible and then easy

**Setting.** The four XOR points of §2.2.2.

**No line works.** The four conditions $b\le0$, $w_2 + b > 0$, $w_1 + b > 0$, $w_1 + w_2 + b\le 0$ are contradictory: the middle two sum to $w_1 + w_2 + 2b > 0$, i.e. $w_1 + w_2 + b > -b\ge 0$.

**One extra feature.** With $\phi = (x_1, x_2, x_1x_2)$, $w = (1, 1, -2)$, $b = -0.5$:

| $x$ | $\phi(x)$ | score $w^{\top}\phi + b$ | prediction | label |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (0,0) | (0,0,0) | −0.5 | 0 | 0 |
| (0,1) | (0,1,0) | +0.5 | 1 | 1 |
| (1,0) | (1,0,0) | +0.5 | 1 | 1 |
| (1,1) | (1,1,1) | −0.5 | 0 | 0 |

**Reading.** Every point has functional margin $0.5$ in absolute score. The geometric distance to the separating plane is $0.5/\lVert w\rVert=0.5/\sqrt6$; calling 0.5 itself a geometric margin would ignore the scale of $w$. A hidden layer can compute the added feature: on binary inputs, $\mathrm{ReLU}(x_1+x_2-1)=x_1x_2$. The final read-out is still linear, but it is linear in a representation that now exposes the needed interaction.

## Worked Example 2.2 — A one-hot read-out is a table

**Setting.** The three-symbol alphabet of Worked Example 1.2 and its add-one smoothed bigram.

Put $W[a, b] = \ln q_1(b\mid a)$. For input `b` (one-hot $(0, 1, 0)$), the scores are $W^{\top}(0,1,0)^{\top} = W[\texttt b] = (\ln\tfrac26, \ln\tfrac16, \ln\tfrac36) = (-1.0986, -1.7918, -0.6931)$. Their softmax is $(\tfrac26, \tfrac16, \tfrac36)$ — exactly row `b` of the table, because the row already sums to one. Adding any constant to a row of $W$ changes nothing, so the linear model has $V(V-1)$ effective parameters: exactly the table's.

**Reading.** One-hot encoding followed by an **unrestricted linear read-out** has no parameters shared by two input rows. The qualifier matters: a one-hot vector can still feed a constrained or multilayer model that shares later parameters. What reproduces the independent count table is this particular representation–read-out pair.

## Worked Example 2.3 — Parameter accounting for longer contexts

**Setting.** $V = 50$. Count models over the previous $k$ characters versus a neural n-gram with $d = 16$, $H = 128$.

| $k$ | possible contexts $V^k$ | contexts seen in the 385 274 training characters | neural n-gram parameters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 50 | 50 | 9 426 |
| 2 | 2 500 | 401 | 11 474 |
| 3 | 125 000 | 2 078 | 13 522 |
| 4 | 6 250 000 | 4 502 | 15 570 |
| 6 | 15 625 000 000 | 12 903 | 19 666 |

The neural count is $Vd + kdH + H + HV + V = 7\,378 + 2\,048k$.

**Reading.** A sparse count implementation stores rows only for observed contexts; under the add-$\alpha$ rule used here, a completely unseen row produces a uniform prediction. The neural model adds 2 048 parameters per extra context position. It *can* transfer to an unseen context when its learned embeddings and shared function place that context near useful observed ones, but it is not guaranteed to predict sensibly. This synthetic corpus is highly repetitive, so the number of observed contexts is especially small; the table shows exponential possibility versus linear neural parameter growth, not a universal coverage rate for natural text.

## Worked Example 2.4 — How wide must the embedding be?

**Setting.** The factorized bigram $z_{ab} = e_a^{\top}Me_b + c_b$ on the spine corpus, trained with Adam (1 500 steps of 4 096 pairs), for several widths $d$.

| $d$ | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 | 50 | count bigram |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| stored trainable values | 154 | 266 | 514 | 1 106 | 2 674 | 5 050 | 2 500 logits (2 450 effective) |
| held-out NLL | 2.65 | 2.37 | 2.21 | **2.15** | 2.15 | 2.15 | 2.12 |

**Reading.** Width 16 comes within 0.03 nats of the count table while storing 1 106 trainable values instead of 2 500 logits. Wider models do not improve under this fixed 1 500-step protocol. That observation alone does not prove that 16 is the table's intrinsic dimension or that longer training would close the final gap: optimization, parameterization and finite-sample effects are entangled. What the experiment does show is that $d$ acts as a capacity knob and that $d=2$ is too restrictive here, losing 0.53 nats relative to the count model.

## Worked Example 2.5 — What the table learned

**Setting.** The $d = 16$ model of Worked Example 2.4. Nearest neighbours by cosine similarity of embeddings.

| query | nearest neighbours (cosine) |
|---|---|
| `3` | `4` 0.98, `2` 0.98, `1` 0.97, `5` 0.97 |
| `T` | `Z` 0.86, `N` 0.85, `S` 0.83, `L` 0.76 |
| `a` | `u` 0.45, `e` 0.39, `A` 0.23 |
| `.` | `,` 0.59 |

**Reading.** The model was told nothing about digits, capitals or vowels; it was only asked to predict the next character. Because the table is tied, an embedding reflects both how a character predicts what follows and how it is predicted from what precedes. Digits occupy almost interchangeable roles in this corpus, so they nearly coincide; capitals that start names cluster; vowels are only weakly similar. These neighbours describe this run's task-specific geometry, not stable semantic categories—another seed, corpus or objective can rotate or reshape it (§2.7.4).

## Worked Example 2.6 — When does sharing pay?

**Setting.** Predict from the previous four characters. The 1 400 stories are split by story into 1 260 training, 70 validation and 70 test stories. For each training prefix, choose the count 4-gram's $\alpha$ from $\{0.003,0.01,0.03,0.1,0.3\}$ on validation only. Compare once on untouched test stories with a neural 4-gram that uses the same fixed training protocol at every $n$ ($d=16$, $H=128$, 2 500 Adam steps of 512 windows, weight decay $10^{-4}$).

| training characters $n$ | 5 000 | 20 000 | 80 000 | 385 274 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| selected count $\alpha$ | 0.003 | 0.003 | 0.01 | 0.01 |
| count 4-gram, test NLL | 1.07 | 0.67 | 0.56 | **0.51** |
| neural 4-gram, test NLL | **0.94** | **0.62** | **0.55** | 0.53 |

**Reading.** At 5 000 characters, the neural model is better by 0.13 nats; at 80 000 the gap is only 0.01; with all training data, the count model is better by 0.02. Sharing lets the model *spend fewer examples per situation*, which matters most in the scarce-data regime. This table does not isolate representation from optimization or model size, and it does not prove where the crossover lies for another corpus. It does show the predicted trend on untouched test stories without selecting $\alpha$ on the reported measurements.

## Worked Example 2.7 — The gradient of a lookup

**Setting.** $V = 3$, $d = 2$, indices $x = (0, 2, 0)$, upstream gradient $G = \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\\5&6\end{pmatrix}$ (one row per looked-up position).

**Scatter-add** (Proposition 2.1): row 0 receives rows 1 and 3 of $G$, row 2 receives row 2:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial E} = \begin{pmatrix}1+5 & 2+6\\ 0 & 0\\ 3 & 4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}6&8\\0&0\\3&4\end{pmatrix} = U^{\top}G,\quad U = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&1\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**The bug.** Writing `dE[x] = G` instead of `np.add.at(dE, x, G)` leaves row 0 equal to $(5, 6)$ — the last write wins — and silently halves the learning signal for every character that occurs twice in a batch. A [gradient check](/atlas/nodes/method-gradient-check/) catches it; a loss curve usually does not.

## Worked Example 2.8 — Counting what a line can do

**Setting.** Four points in general position in the plane, and affine classifiers $\mathrm{sign}(w^{\top}x + b)$.

**Cover's count** (Theorem 2.3, §2.5.2): $N$ points in general position in $\mathbb{R}^p$ admit $C(N, p+1) = 2\sum_{k=0}^{p}\binom{N-1}{k}$ affinely separable labellings. With $N = 4$, $p = 2$: $C = 2\big(\binom30 + \binom31 + \binom32\big) = 2(1 + 3 + 3) = 14$ of the $2^4 = 16$ possible labellings.

**Reading.** For the four XOR points, the two missing labellings are XOR and its complement. Lifting to $\phi=(x_1,x_2,x_1x_2)$ makes the four points affinely independent in $\mathbb R^3$, where $C(4,4)=16$: every labelling is separable. Added dimensions therefore increase the number of label patterns a linear read-out *can express*. They do not guarantee that optimization will find a useful one; the same capacity that can expose a signal can also fit noise (Unit 01, §1.5.4).
